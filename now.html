Now

Building

Name That Face is sitting in Apple's review queue — my first app submitted to the App Store. It turns a PDF of headshots into flashcards, so you can meet a room full of strangers on Monday and greet all of them by name on Friday. I built it for myself at a theater conservatory, where a new cohort arrives a few times a year and somebody hands me a sheet of faces. Then another teacher asked for it, which is the moment a tool for one person has to start working on somebody else's phone.

Most of the work of getting there wasn't the idea. It was everything the idea doesn't cover: the extraction assumed my PDFs and fell apart on other people's layouts, the first screen never said what the app was for, and the whole thing came unglued at the largest accessibility text size. None of that is visible in a demo. All of it is the difference between something I use and something anyone can.

Before that, the Burpee Counter — a fullscreen rep counter for filming a daily 100-burpee video, which counts by voice and is designed to survive the mic mishearing me at rep 70. The quieter obsession is the infrastructure underneath everything: a personal wiki that maintains itself, running on an always-on Mac Mini. That story lives on the AI page now.

Doing

100 burpees. Every day. On camera. It started as a fitness thing and became a shipping-discipline thing: the daily video needed a counter, the counter needed voice, the voice needed to handle failure. Nothing teaches product like being your own most annoyed user seven days a week.

Located

New York City. Specifically: wherever the wifi is decent and the background noise is tolerable, which narrows it down to about four places.

Reading

Whatever's open in the most recent browser tab I didn't mean to leave open. Also some actual books, occasionally.

Thinking about

What one person can ship now that the bottleneck isn't typing speed. The answer keeps moving, and I keep documenting it. Still convinced the play is specificity — small, sharp tools for real people with real problems — and increasingly convinced the second skill is knowing exactly how much to trust the machine, and when.
